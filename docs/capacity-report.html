What was actually run. Every figure on this page comes from drishtiCapacityDES.m, an entity-level discrete-event simulation of 96,879 screened patients and 190,180 images, executed in MATLAB R2026a.

The companion Simulink flow model, buildDRishtiSimulinkModel.m, was written but not executed — the build machine had base MATLAB only, with no Simulink installed. Validate it with compareSimulinkToDES.m before relying on its output.

The pipeline, and how hard each stage works

Five stages, one shared six-hour window

Every site captures inside the same session, so demand arrives as a daily burst. Uplink and compute run around the clock and drain it; technicians, graders and the specialist clinic only work their shift.

The automated pass is effectively instantaneous against clinic tempo: a median of 0.4 minutes from capture to graded result, and 100% of patients get a result before they leave the camp. What patients wait for is people, not silicon.

1. Average utilisation hides the compute requirement

One worker at 11 s/image sits at 9.8% of a 24-hour day — which suggests enormous headroom. It has far less than that. Because all eight sites capture in the same six hours, the number that governs queueing is utilisation during the session: 39.3%, four times higher.

That distinction decides whether same-session delivery survives. Once session utilisation passes 100%, the queue stops draining before the camp closes:

One worker holds to about 24 s/image; two workers to about 48 s. Past that, same-session delivery collapses from 100% to 22% while the 24-hour utilisation figure still reads a reassuring 35%.

The 11 s estimate is read off the algorithms, not measured — 24 matched-filter convolutions at 1024 px, a 51×51 medfilt2, a large-sigma imgaussfilt, and bwskel with a per-segment tortuosity loop. It is the least reliable input on this page, and it is also the easiest to fix: benchmark segmentRetina on the actual district hardware before buying anything.

Note where the cost sits. The classical segmentation dominates; CNN inference at 224×224 is a rounding error beside it. A GPU buys you very little here.

2. The specialist clinic is what breaks as the programme grows

Scale the programme and four of the five resources stay comfortable. Ophthalmologist slots do not — they cross 100% somewhere between 100,000 and 150,000 patients, and past that the referral backlog grows without bound.

The cliff is steep because the resource is lumpy: half an FTE produces a 2,242-case backlog and a four-month wait, one FTE produces essentially none. There is no graceful degradation to plan around — the specialist session is either there or it is not.

3. Bandwidth is a solved problem above half a megabit

Rural connectivity is the usual objection to telemedicine screening. Here it does not bind. Two design choices do the work: the quality gate runs at the edge, so rejected images never travel, and transfer is store-and-forward, so a backlog built during the session drains afterwards.

Recommendation

The baseline over-buys compute and under-buys camps

Minimum configuration meeting: unmet demand under 2%, every resource below 85%, at least 95% of reports inside the SLA, specialist p95 wait under 14 days.

Verified as a combined configuration

97,450 patients screened against a 100,000 target — 1.3% of demand unseated, down from 2.1%. Every resource under 76%. Compute at 39.3% of session hours on a single worker. p95 report turnaround 0.25 hours; 100% of patients still receive a result before leaving the camp.

Eight sites is the quiet failure in the baseline: it leaves 2.1% of demand unseated, because a camp that runs out of session hours turns people away rather than queueing them. Ten sites recovers most of that. The two freed compute workers and the freed grader FTE cost more, in unscreened patients, than they were ever going to save.

Caveats

What would change these answers

  • Per-image compute time (11 s, estimated). Governs the whole compute conclusion. Measurable on your hardware this afternoon.
  • CNN/rule disagreement rate (22%, assumed). Sets grader load directly. The checkpoint currently in the repository is a three-epoch demo fine-tune, so the real rate is unknown and probably worse.
  • Referable rate (7%, from literature). Sets specialist load, which is the binding constraint — so this is the assumption most worth replacing with district data.
  • Loss to follow-up is not modelled. The referral stage books appointments; it does not model whether patients attend. In practice that is a large effect and it works in the opposite direction to everything here.
  • Turned-away patients are not rescheduled. Where appointment booking exists, achievable throughput is higher than modelled.
